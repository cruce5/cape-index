Superhero box office  ·  2000–2026

The Cape Index

Every live-action Marvel and DC superhero film since 2000 that is part of a franchise, and what each one made. Worldwide grosses come from Box Office Mojo, checked one film at a time. Any number on the page switches to 2025 dollars.

By 🏴‍☠️ Bill Yost

It also works as a set of worked examples. Turn on and every chart explains why it’s built the way it is: the form, the colour, and what it can’t tell you. The switch also lives in the control bar.

    Act I  ·  The scoreboard

    how big, and who
    § 01  /  MARVEL v DC

    Marvel Studios has out-grossed every DC film combined, nearly three to one

    Worldwide gross by release year
    Stacked by universe. 2018 and 2019 were the high-water mark, near $6.8B each. The 2021–22 rebound stopped around $3.9B; 2023 onward has settled near $2.3B, roughly a third of the peak.
    Design note
    Question
    How has total superhero box office moved year to year, and how does each year split between the studios?
    Why this form
    A stacked column reads as one total per year (the height) and a split within it (the segments). Separate lines would bury the “how big was the whole year” question; a grouped column would make the year total hard to see.
    Choices here
    Segments are held apart by a 2 px gap in the page colour, not a stroke, a border is ink that isn’t data. Universe order is fixed, so a colour never jumps position between years. Y-axis labels sit flush left, close to the title.
    Watch for
    Stacking hides the individual films. Good for the total and rough shares; useless for “which 2021 Marvel film was that.” That’s what the table is for.
    Spread of worldwide grosses, by universe
    Box spans the 25th–75th percentile; the line is the median; dots are individual films. Marvel’s median film out-grosses roughly three of every four DC films.
    Design note
    Question
    Beyond the averages, how are each universe’s grosses actually spread out?
    Why this form
    A box plot shows the middle 50%, the median and the tails at once. An average alone would hide that DC’s range is both wider and lower. The dots put every film back on the chart so the summary isn’t a black box.
    Choices here
    The line is the median, not the mean, one $2.8B film drags a mean around. DCU has two films, so it gets dots and a median tick only; a quartile box on a handful of films is theatre. Elseworlds, at six, just clears the bar for a box, so read its quartiles loosely.
    Watch for
    With this few films per group, a single film moves the quartiles a lot. Read it as “roughly,” not “precisely.”
    § 02  /  TREEMAP

    Only three films have passed $2 billion, and all three are Marvel

    Every film is a rectangle, sized by worldwide gross. The three biggest are all Marvel: two Avengers films, and Brand New Day, which is still in theatres. DC’s ceiling, Aquaman at $1.15B, sits below ten separate Marvel films.

    Design note
    Question
    Where is the money concentrated across all 91 films at once?
    Why this form
    A treemap fits every film on one screen with area doing the work. A 91-row bar chart would need a long scroll and lose the sense of proportion. It’s built for “a few things are huge, most are not.”
    Choices here
    Squarified layout keeps rectangles near square so their areas stay comparable. A label appears only where it fits with padding, a clipped title is worse than none. Colour is universe identity, never a value ramp.
    Watch for
    People judge area badly, long thin rectangles especially. Use it for the gestalt; use the tooltip or the table for the exact number.

    Act II  ·  Over time

    cadence, scale, budgets
    § 03  /  BY RELEASE DATE

    Since 2020, no year has produced more than one billion-dollar film

    Each film on its release date, sized by worldwide gross. Through 2019 the pattern was a few films a year, several of them enormous; 2018 and 2019 alone had seven billion-dollar films between them. Since 2021 the output is about the same, six or seven films a year, but the films are smaller and the big bubbles thin out to one a year at most. Brand New Day, still in release, is 2026’s.

    Design note
    Question
    When did each film come out and how big was it, without films from the same summer hiding each other?
    Why this form
    A plain timeline stacks dots on top of each other around each release window. The beeswarm nudges them apart so every film stays visible at its real date; the vertical pile-up at a date is how many films landed near it.
    Choices here
    Circle area is proportional to gross, the radius scales with the square root, so the big films aren’t exaggerated. Each new circle is pushed to the nearest open spot. Vertical position on its own means nothing.
    Watch for
    The vertical position of any one circle is just where it landed in the packing; only the density reads. Don’t treat height as a second variable.
    § 04  /  SCATTER

    Marvel has cleared $1 billion twelve times. DC four times, Fox and Sony never

    Worldwide gross by release date. Above the billion-dollar line sit four DC films (Aquaman, Joker, and both of Nolan’s Dark Knight follow-ups); everything else up there is Marvel Studios. Below it, the whole cloud sinks after 2022, with Brand New Day the lone exception so far.

    Design note
    Question
    Is there a trend in film size over 25 years, and which films broke away from the pack?
    Why this form
    Two continuous axes, date and gross, is the textbook scatter. Reference lines at $1B and $2B give the eye something to measure against.
    Choices here
    The palette is a colour-blind-safe set, checked pair by pair. Five chromatic hues already can’t all stay distinct under every kind of colour blindness, so a sixth is a non-starter. Elseworlds (DC’s six films outside any shared universe) is drawn in grey, and every universe also carries a shape: circle MCU, diamond Fox, plus Sony, square DCEU, triangle DCU, cross Elseworlds. Only the six biggest films are labelled; a label on all 91 is noise.
    Watch for
    No trend line. Fitting one here would invent a relationship the data doesn’t support, the story is the spread and the outliers, not a slope.
    § 05  /  THE BUDGET ARMS RACE

    Production budgets roughly doubled over the 2000s, then flattened near $200M

    The median production budget by release year. It climbed from around $100M in the early 2000s to about $200M by 2012, and has mostly held between $150M and $225M since, dipping in years carried by cheaper films (2015, 2020, 2024). The first X-Men cost $75M in 2000; a mid-tier tentpole now runs $200M, and the biggest Avengers films clear $350M. Flip to 2025 dollars to see how much of that is real growth.

    Design note
    Question
    Have production budgets actually climbed once you strip out inflation?
    Why this form
    One measure over time is a line. The faint dots behind it are the individual films, so you can see the spread the line is summarising. Each year’s marker is sized by how many films sit behind its median.
    Choices here
    The line is the median budget per year, the mean would swing with a single $350M Avengers. One line, not four: split by universe and the yearly counts get too small to read.
    Watch for
    The early-2000s points each rest on one or two films, so the line is noisy there, hence the small markers. And budgets are the softest number on the site: studios don’t disclose them, so anything not straight from Box Office Mojo is an estimate, marked est. in the ledger.

    Act III  ·  Beyond the gross

    profit, legs, reviews, geography
    § 06  /  DID IT MAKE MONEY?

    A third of these films never cleared their break-even point

    Each film’s worldwide gross against an estimated break-even of 2.5× its production budget. Profit to the right, loss to the left. The Marvels is the widest miss; Joker the widest hit, at nearly 20× its $55M budget.

    Design note
    Question
    Which films actually cleared their break-even point, and by how much?
    Why this form
    A diverging bar anchored at zero puts profit right, loss left, and sorts the whole slate best to worst in one read. Gross alone can’t answer “did it make money”, a $400M film is a hit or a bomb depending on its budget.
    Choices here
    Zero is break-even, estimated at 2.5 × budget. Bar colour and sign carry the outcome, green/right made money, orange/left lost it, and every value label sits in one column clear of the film names. The universe rides along as a chip on the title.
    Watch for
    Break-even is a rule of thumb, not studio accounting, home video, streaming and TV deals aren’t in it. Trust the ranking more than the exact dollar figure.
    § 07  /  LEGS

    Some films made most of their money in three days; others kept selling for months

    For each film, its opening weekend and its full domestic run, connected. A long line is a film with legs: Aquaman finished at 4.9× its opening. A short one is front-loaded: Joker: Folie à Deux managed 1.5×.

    Design note
    Question
    How much of each film’s domestic run came from its opening weekend versus everything after?
    Why this form
    A dumbbell connects two related numbers, opening weekend and lifetime domestic, for each film, sorted by the multiplier (lifetime ÷ opening) so the leggy films rise to the top.
    Why a log axis
    On a linear dollar axis the connector length would be dollars added after opening, not the ratio, a $260M opener that triples looks longer than a $30M opener that quadruples. A log axis makes the pixel length equal to the multiplier, which is the thing being ranked.
    Watch for
    Big openings and long legs aren’t opposites, Endgame had both. A huge multiple on a tiny platform release isn’t the same phenomenon as a wide release with staying power.
    § 08  /  REVIEWS & REVENUE

    Reviews barely move with box office, and critics and audiences often part ways

    Two questions: do better-reviewed films make more money, and where do critics and audiences split?

    Rotten Tomatoes critic score vs worldwide gross
    One point per film. If reviews drove grosses, this would slope up. It mostly doesn’t.
    Design note
    Question
    Do better reviews go with bigger box office?
    Why this form
    Two continuous variables, one point per film: a scatter. The question is whether there’s a pattern, and the honest answer is “barely.”
    Choices here
    Deliberately no trend line. The correlation is weak; a line would imply something predictable, which this isn’t. The spread is the finding.
    Watch for
    Reviews and gross are both downstream of the same thing: franchise heat, timing, spend. A scatter shows association, never cause.
    Critics vs audiences
    RT critic score against IMDb user rating, both on 0–100. On the diagonal they agree; the widest splits are Sony’s Venom-adjacent films (Kraven, Morbius, Venom itself) plus Elektra, Batman v Superman and Dark Phoenix, where the critic score craters but the audience rating holds up.
    Design note
    Question
    Where do critics and the audience disagree, and who liked it more?
    Why this form
    Plot one score against the other and draw the 45° line where they’d match. Distance from the line is the size of the disagreement; the side tells you who was warmer.
    Choices here
    Both scores are put on 0–100 so the parity line is a true diagonal. The line is drawn thin and grey. It’s a reference, not data.
    Watch for
    RT critic % (share of positive reviews) and an IMDb rating (average score) aren’t the same kind of measure. The diagonal is a visual aid, not a claim that the scales are equivalent.
    § 09  /  DOMESTIC v OVERSEAS

    Overseas is the majority, but the split swings from 43% to 75%

    Each film split into domestic and overseas gross, sortable by the overseas share. The most domestic are recent DC films, Superman and Supergirl, at 43% overseas; the furthest-travelled is the first Venom at 75%.

    Design note
    Question
    For each film, how much of the gross came from North America versus everywhere else?
    Why this form
    Part-to-whole, one film per row. A pie per film would be 91 pies; a stacked bar per row shows the split and lets you scan the whole list.
    Choices here
    Domestic is the solid bar, overseas the same colour at low opacity, one hue keeps the universe identity while the fill separates the two parts. Two sort orders: by total gross, or normalised so you compare shares directly.
    Watch for
    “Overseas” lumps very different markets together. China alone swings some of these films by ten points.

    Act IV  ·  The roster

    who shows up where
    § 10  /  WHO'S IN WHICH FILM

    Marvel built a shared cast; DC kept starting over

    Every costumed hero and villain, and the films they appear in, in release order. A filled cell is an appearance. The Avengers films are the vertical stripes; DC’s rows barely connect.

    Design note
    Question
    Which characters recur, and how connected is each universe’s filmography?
    Why this form
    A presence grid, character × film, is the honest way to show set membership across many items. Density and gaps do the talking; no summary stat captures “the roster hangs together” as directly.
    Choices here
    Rows are the 26 most-recurring characters, ordered by appearance count. Columns are all 91 films in release order, with year ticks. A recast is one character (Hulk = Norton then Ruffalo); a passed-on mantle is not (Sam Wilson’s Falcon and his Captain America are separate rows, as are Natasha’s and Yelena’s Black Widow).
    Watch for
    “Codenamed” is a judgement call. Plain-name roles are out even when the person is central: Nick Fury, Pepper Potts, Alfred. And a character counts under a codename only from the film they take it up: Thaddeus Ross is five films of General Ross and one of Red Hulk, so he shows here once. Cast lists are from Wikipedia and skew to billed roles, so quick cameos are undercounted.
    Codenamed characters per film
    How many heroes and villains each film puts on screen: in release order on a wide screen, ranked on a phone. Avengers: Endgame tops out at 33.
    § 11  /  MOST-TRAVELLED

    Spider-Man has been in twelve films, more than any other character by a clear margin

    Characters ranked by how many films they turn up in, from Raimi’s trilogy and the Amazing Spider-Man pair to the MCU run. Iron Man is next at nine, Wolverine at eight; Batman, at seven across the Nolan, DCEU and Reeves continuities, tops DC. The long tail, 130-odd characters with a single appearance, is trimmed; the full list is in “Show data”.

    Design note
    Question
    Who carries the most cross-film weight?
    Why this form
    A lollipop, not a bar: the counts are small integers (1–9), so a dot on a light stem reads the value cleanly without a heavy block of ink for “3”.
    Choices here
    Sorted by count, coloured by the universe the character appears in most. Single-appearance characters are cut, there are ~130 of them and they’d bury the signal.
    Watch for
    Each identity is one person here. “Captain America” is Steve Rogers; Sam Wilson’s turn is its own row, as are Yelena’s Black Widow and Shuri’s Black Panther. A codename counts from the film the character takes it up, not before, so Red Hulk and Mighty Thor sit at one film each.

    The tempting mistakes

    beautiful charts that don’t help
    § 12  /  ANTI-PATTERNS

    Every chart down here is accurate. Not one of them earns its place

    Eight charts, built from the same 91 films as everything above. Each one is quick to make, easy on the eyes, and quietly useless. They always use every film and nominal dollars; the universe filter and the $ toggle in the control bar don’t reach them.

    ✗ Dual axis
    Budget and gross on one chart, on two y-axes
    Tempting
    One picture, two related trends, and they seem to move with each other. It reads as a single, finished story.
    But
    Neither axis has a meaningful zero, and sliding either one moves where the bars and line cross. From the same numbers you can make budget appear to lead gross, lag it, or drive it.
    Instead
    Two panels, or index both series to their year-2000 value so they share one scale. §05 keeps budget on its own axis for exactly this reason.
    ✗ Pie chart
    Every film a wedge, sized by worldwide gross
    Tempting
    A pie says “here is the whole thing, carved up.” Banded by universe, this one also happens to look great.
    But
    You can’t compare two wedges that aren’t adjacent, can’t rank the small ones, and can’t read a single value. Roughly half the films are slivers.
    Instead
    A sorted bar, or the treemap in §02: still area, but sorted, aligned to an edge, and labelled where a label fits.
    ✗ Truncated axis
    Average gross by universe, y-axis starting well above zero
    Tempting
    The gap between Marvel and the rest finally “pops.” The chart looks decisive.
    But
    Bar length is the encoding. Chop the baseline and the proportions break: this chart shows Marvel around 3× DCEU; the real ratio is 1.9×.
    Instead
    Bars start at zero, always. If the small differences are the point, use a dot plot. §01 does this.
    ✗ Cumulative total
    Running total of worldwide gross, 2000 to 2026
    Tempting
    It only goes up and to the right. It feels like momentum, an industry on a tear.
    But
    A running total can’t go down. This shape is guaranteed no matter what the last five years looked like, and it buries the fact that annual output has fallen by two thirds since 2019.
    Instead
    Plot each year’s value, not the running sum. §01’s year chart shows the decline this view erases.
    ✗ Trend line
    Critic score against gross, with a fitted line
    Tempting
    The line turns a shapeless cloud into a claim: better reviews, bigger box office. The R² label looks like proof.
    But
    The fit is weak (R²) and most of it is a few films that are both acclaimed and enormous. The line still invites you to read “reviews drive gross” straight off the slope, which the spread doesn’t support.
    Instead
    No line. Let the scatter be a scatter. §04 and §08 leave it out for this reason.
    ✗ Ratio ranking
    Films ranked by gross divided by production budget
    Tempting
    “Return on budget” sounds like the grown-up, follow-the-money metric, and the leaderboard is full of surprises.
    But
    It’s a ratio with budget on the bottom, so it rewards being cheap. A small film that does fine outranks a giant that prints money in actual dollars, and marketing spend isn’t in it.
    Instead
    Rank by absolute profit (§06), or plot the ratio against budget so the small-budget tilt is visible.
    ✗ Radar chart
    A six-metric “fingerprint” per universe
    Tempting
    It looks like a character-select screen. You want to say one universe is more “well-rounded” than another.
    But
    The shape’s area, the thing the eye compares, depends on the order of the spokes and how each was scaled. Reorder the axes and the “winner” changes. Six different units don’t belong on shared radii.
    Instead
    Small multiples: one plain bar chart per metric, same scale across universes.
    ✗ Rainbow ramp
    Gross by universe and year, on a rainbow colour scale
    Tempting
    “Heat” looks like data density, and more colours feels like more information.
    But
    A rainbow isn’t ordered. Is yellow more than green? More than cyan? You keep rechecking the key, and the colour jumps imply thresholds that aren’t there. It’s also not colour-blind-safe.
    Instead
    One hue, light to dark. Lightness is perceptually ordered, so magnitude reads without a key. It’s also why this page never uses a colour ramp for a value at all: identity gets a hue, magnitude gets position or length.

    Reference

    the raw table & the method
    § 13  /  THE LEDGER

    The whole thing, sortable

    Every film and every field behind the charts above. Click a column header to sort. Mult. is domestic gross ÷ opening weekend: how long a film kept selling. ROI is worldwide gross ÷ production budget.

    Design note
    Why this form
    A sorted table is the most information-dense view there is, and it’s the accessibility backbone: every value a chart encodes with position or colour is a plain number here.
    Choices here
    Click any header to sort. Money columns use a monospaced figure so the digits line up. Universe is a colour chip, not a colour-coded row, the text still has to work on its own.
    Watch for
    Sorting by a derived column (Mult., ROI) rewards small films with freak ratios. Read those next to the raw gross.
    § 14  /  METHOD

    How this was built

    Sources

    Worldwide, domestic and overseas grosses and opening weekends come from Box Office Mojo title pages, read one at a time. Scores are from OMDb: Rotten Tomatoes (critics), Metacritic, IMDb.

    Budgets are the production budget, never a marketing-inclusive figure. Box Office Mojo where it lists a clean one; otherwise the widely-cited number, marked est. Roughly half are estimates, and studios don’t publish the real figure, so treat any single ROI as soft and the ranking as the point.

    Definitions

    2025 $ rescales every gross and budget by annual CPI-U. Break-even is estimated at 2.5× the production budget, the standard proxy that folds in marketing and the exhibitor split; home video, streaming and licensing are not in it, so a “loss” here is a theatrical shortfall, not a studio write-down.

    Still in release films (marked in release) carry grosses as of early September 2026 and will climb; they are left out of the profit tallies until their runs end.

    Scope

    One rule: every live-action theatrical Marvel or DC superhero film whose continuity began in 2000 or later and that is part of a multi-film continuity, meaning a shared universe, a trilogy, or a film with a theatrical sequel released or in production. That is the MCU; the DCEU and James Gunn’s DCU; DC’s standalone films (Nolan’s Dark Knight trilogy, both Jokers, The Batman); 20th Century Fox’s Marvel films (the X-Men series, the 2005 Fantastic Four and Rise of the Silver Surfer, Daredevil and Elektra); and Sony’s Marvel films (Raimi’s Spider-Man trilogy, The Amazing Spider-Man 1 and 2, the Venom-led SSU, and both Ghost Riders). Deadpool & Wolverine counts as MCU, not Fox.

    Out: continuities that began before 2000 (Burton’s Batman, Donner’s Superman and its 2006 coda Superman Returns, the Blade trilogy); anything animated (the Spider-Verse trilogy, DC League of Super-Pets); one-and-done films with no sequel, which have no franchise arc to compare: Catwoman, Constantine, Green Lantern, Jonah Hex, Watchmen, Ang Lee’s Hulk, the 2015 Fantastic Four (a reboot that never got its sequel or its X-Men crossover), both Punishers (2008’s is a reboot, not a sequel); and creator-owned books Marvel merely published (Kick-Ass). A later multiverse cameo (Tobey Maguire in No Way Home, Michael Keaton in The Flash) doesn’t pull an excluded film back in.

    § 15  /  BUILD LOG

    How this actually went

    The rest of the site is worked examples of finished charts. This is the messy one: the brief, every follow-up, every bug, and the design calls behind them. If you build data things, the useful part is usually the part nobody publishes.

    The brief

    It started as a maintenance job. Verify a Tableau Public workbook, Marvel / DC Box Office, against Box Office Mojo: 50 films, six columns, movie / date / franchise / domestic / international / total. Then extend it with the films that had come out since, and rebuild the seven-tab Tableau suite as one self-contained dark-first HTML page.

    It ended at 91 films across six groups, twenty-odd follow-ups, a scope rule that took three tries, and a cast scraper that turned out to be lying.

    The pipeline

    Adding a film means appending it to a list with a pinned IMDb id, then: scrape Box Office Mojo for worldwide / domestic / overseas / opening weekend, one title page at a time; pull Rotten Tomatoes, Metacritic and IMDb from OMDb; read the Wikipedia Cast section for the roster; derive the rest (break-even at 2.5× budget, ROI, domestic multiplier, every figure also rescaled to 2025 dollars by CPI-U); slim it to a compact JSON; inline everything into the single HTML file.

    Charts are hand-written inline <svg>. No charting library, so nothing on the page is a default that slipped through unquestioned.

    Every follow-up, in order
    1. “Red Hulk’s been in four movies? No way, bud.” Rebuilt how a character is identified across films.
    2. Show which films a character appears in, from the hover card itself.
    3. “Get rid of all the em dashes everywhere. Pronto.”
    4. Dead space at the top of the mobile scorecards → a full mobile pass.
    5. A launch-readiness audit, desktop and mobile treated as separate reviews. One brief: are all the hovercards as useful as they could be?
    6. Add a System / Auto theme option. Add Iron Monger to the roster.
    7. Add Sony’s Spider-Man Universe. That made it a fifth category and broke the colour scheme.
    8. “Marvel being red isn’t a hard rule. Is there a five-palette safe option?” Dropped the red/blue franchise convention entirely.
    9. Add all-in profit to the §01 scorecards.
    10. “Weird spacing issue here”: an empty rectangle beside the masthead text.
    11. The §03 bubble chart was rough on mobile. Several iterations before it landed.
    12. “Anything the box office subreddit would roast me for?”
    13. A byline linking to LinkedIn, and a footer credit.
    14. “AUDIT ALL THE THINGS”: two reference cards were misaligned and a studio was missing from the footer.
    15. “Should Fox’s X-Men be in here?” Added the Fox era and The Amazing Spider-Man 1 & 2.
    16. “Would reconsidering the 2008 start blow the view to hell?” Moved the whole set back to 2000.
    17. The inclusion rule sprang a leak → the full-franchise rule. Nolan, Raimi, Ghost Rider, Daredevil, the 2005 Fantastic Four. 92 films.
    18. “Supergirl is out on digital, so it’s basically done in theatres.” Marked it final; the DCU scorecard went from +$56M to −$317M.
    19. “Adversarial-audit the ever-living hell out of this.” That is where the cast scraper got caught.
    20. Collapse / expand on the four tallest charts, collapsed by default.
    21. “Would a viz snob look at this and go ‘hmm, nice’?” Treemap labels and the budget markers got a polish pass.
    22. Bought capeindex.com. Wired the custom domain, and a 301 from thecapeindex.com; every share URL now points there.
    23. “A profit-per-movie metric on the scoreboard, if the box office subreddit would like it.” Added as theatrical P/L per completed film, muted where too few films have finished to average one honestly.
    24. “A collection of anti-patterns. Beautiful charts that don’t help.” §12: eight of them, built from the real data, each with what makes it tempting and what to do instead.
    25. “Adversarially audit this entire thing. Pull no punches.” Eight pieces of copy had rotted out from under the data (a headline that was false against its own chart, notes still describing a 57-film set). And the 2015 Fantastic Four turned out to be a one-and-done sitting in the Fox bucket, in breach of the rule. “Pull it.” 91 films.
    26. “Some squint-worthy text on a number of visualizations. We have to build for mobile here.” Every chart drew into a fixed 480-unit box that a phone then shrank to about 350 pixels, so an 11-point label landed at 8. The charts now measure their container and draw at one unit per pixel. “Still small. Bump everything a point or two.” Every chart label on a phone now gets two more points after render; nothing is under 12.
    27. “Do all of our white / grey text hues pass contrast on the dark background?” They do: the greys sit at 5.0 to 8.4 to 1 on every dark surface, AA or better. The audit found the misses elsewhere: two coloured axis labels in §12, the light-theme profit and loss colours on tooltip backgrounds by a hair, a token the forced-light block had never received at all, and the treemap: white labels on the MCU green were 3 to 1. Each cell now picks dark or white ink by measuring its own fill.
    28. “We are way too scrolly now. People will never go to the bottom. Implement some structure.” Fifteen sections became six tabs, one per act, with a contents list under the masthead that carries every finding as a headline. Someone who reads only the contents still gets all fifteen.
    29. “Put the mistakes before Reference.” Last is where things go to die. Sections renumbered to match. “I didn’t know to swipe.” The tab strip clipped on a phone with nothing saying there was more. Now it fades at the edge while it can still scroll, and every tab ends in a Back and a Next button named for where they go.
    30. “On mobile, can the filters collapse into an expandable bar that follows the scroll?” They stick under the tabs now, folded to one row that says what is selected, and open on tap.
    31. “I like the scorecards left to right instead of stacked. Do an entire viz audit of both desktop and mobile, with haste.” The three masthead tiles sit side by side on a phone. A script walked every chart at 412 and 1200 pixels measuring label boxes against each other and against the chart edge: ten collisions, from a /usr/bin/bash tick sitting on a ’00 year label to two bar labels printing on top of each other. All ten fixed; both widths now report zero.
    32. “Design notes and the filters are too buried now. And the copy says ‘up in the toolbar’ when the bar is below it.” The masthead line is now the switch itself; the phone bar carries a Notes pill outside the fold; the notes control turns gold when on, everywhere it appears.
    33. “This chart is tough on mobile.” The characters-per-film chart drew 91 bars three pixels wide on a phone. It is a ranked list there now, top twelve and a fold; the wide screen keeps the timeline. Its note also still said Infinity War topped out at 22, from before the cast scraper was fixed, and the axis stopped at 20 under a bar that reached 33. The number is read from the data now.
    The bugs
    Red Hulk in four films
    The canonicaliser counted every plain Thaddeus Ross credit as the codename. Rule now: a character counts under a codename only from the film they take it up, and a later transformation identity (Red Hulk, Mighty Thor) needs the explicit Name / Codename alias in the credit. Ross is five films of a general and one of a monster, so he shows once.
    A duplicate ghost film
    Joker: Folie à Deux was in the source list as Folie a Deux (plain a) and on Box Office Mojo as Folie à Deux (accented). Two records, one with no universe, showing up as an UNKNOWN entry. Fix: match the source of truth’s spelling exactly, delete the stale scrape, re-run.
    A find-and-replace that missed half the CSS
    The dark-to-light colour overrides live in two rule blocks with different indentation: one under @media (prefers-color-scheme: light), one under [data-theme="light"]. A “replace all” on a token value hit one and not the other, so the manual toggle and the OS setting disagreed about a colour until both were patched.
    An unfinished film poisoning the profit math
    Supergirl, still in theatres at the time, sat at about $126M on a $200M budget and dragged the whole DCU scorecard to −$317M, the single most roastable number on the page. Fix then: hold in-release films out of every profit tally. Fix now: once its run actually ended, put it back. The −$317M is real.
    A title collision, and a self-healing bug that wasn’t
    The 2005 and 2015 Fantastic Four films are both literally titled “Fantastic Four”, which collided on the join key, so both were disambiguated with a year. Worse: the scraper seeds its worklist from the previous build’s output, so renaming a film silently regenerated the old key and produced a phantom UNKNOWN film until the stale key was deleted by hand.
    The cast scraper was quietly dropping people
    Found by asking a small question during the audit: why is the Joker credited in only one film? Five separate causes. (1) An HTML comment sitting between “as” and the character name, and a strip that was too greedy about it, so Doctor Strange vanished from four films. (2) the Joker: the lowercase article meant the string never matched the canonical name, so Ledger’s and Leto’s Jokers went uncounted. (3) Dr. Stephen Strange: an honorific the lookup didn’t strip. (4) J.A.R.V.I.S. and Vision: a dual credit where neither half matched alone. (5) The big one: pages that list returning cast in a prose sentence (Several actors reprise their roles…) rather than a bullet list were never parsed, so Falcon, Scarlet Witch, Winter Soldier, Star-Lord and a dozen others were missing their Endgame appearance.
    …and the fix
    Strip HTML comments and citations before matching. A case-insensitive lookup that also drops articles and honorifics. Split credits on “and”. A guarded prose scan that only reads a paragraph if it carries a reprisal cue, only takes clean [[Actor]] as [[Character]] pairs, and skips anything preceded by “footage of” or “was cast”. A short, hand-checked overrides file for the last few stars buried in prose. Recovered counts: Joker 1→4, Magneto 5→7, Doctor Strange 2→5, and every Endgame reprisal.
    The design calls
    Titles state the finding
    Every section headline is the takeaway, not a label. Marvel Studios has out-grossed every DC film combined, nearly three to one, never Gross by universe. If a title can’t say something, the chart probably shouldn’t exist.
    The palette was computed, not chosen
    The red/blue franchise convention is gone, because red read too close to pink for the person who has to look at this daily. The replacement is a colour-blind-safe set checked with a script, pair by pair, in both light and dark. Established along the way: five or more saturated categorical hues cannot pass an all-pairs colour-blindness test in both modes. Confirmed against two published palettes. Every universe also carries a shape, so identity is never colour alone.
    The sixth colour is grey on purpose
    Once the Fox films arrived, six distinct chromatic colours was not possible. Elseworlds, the standalone films attached to no franchise engine, is drawn in grey. It fails the palette validator’s chroma floor by design: grey is the “doesn’t belong to a machine” signal.
    Things this site never does
    No dual-axis charts: two measures of different scale become two charts. No trend line where the data doesn’t support one (reviews vs. gross; film size over time), because fitting one invents a relationship. No mean where a median is honest; one $2.8B film drags a mean around.
    Why the legs chart uses a log axis
    On a linear dollar axis, the connector length would be dollars added after opening weekend, which flatters big films. On a log axis the pixel length equals the multiplier, which is the thing being ranked.
    The treemap is a compromise, and says so
    People judge area badly, and long thin rectangles worst of all. It stays because it delivers the “wall of green” in one glance. But a clipped title reads as a mistake, so a film’s name appears only when it fits the cell uncut; otherwise you get the gross and the tooltip.
    The budget line admits its weak spots
    Each year’s marker is sized by how many films sit behind its median. The early-2000s points often rest on a single film, so they’re small, and the eye discounts that stretch without being told to.
    The table is the backbone
    Every value a chart encodes with position, length or colour is a plain sortable number in §13. The charts are the argument; the table is the receipts.
    The scope question, which took three tries

    This was the hardest editorial call, and the first two answers were wrong.

    Try one: start at 2008, the year the MCU launched. Clean, until the Fox X-Men run went in. X-Men: Days of Future Past is a direct sequel to the 2000 original, so keeping the sequel and cutting the original was indefensible. The start moved to 2000.

    Try two: include an older continuity if an actor later reprised the role. This collapsed within minutes. Deadpool & Wolverine, The Flash and No Way Home have brought back nearly every dormant franchise on screen. A cameo can’t be the test, or the scope is everything.

    Try three, which held: every live-action Marvel or DC superhero film whose continuity began in 2000 or later and that is part of a multi-film continuity, meaning a shared universe, a trilogy, or a film with a theatrical sequel. One-and-done films are out; with no sequel there is no franchise arc to compare. It’s mechanical, it doesn’t leak, and §14 names every exclusion so the argument is on the page, not in someone’s head.

    How it’s checked

    Every change is verified by driving a headless browser and reading the rendered page (element counts, computed styles, the actual numbers in the actual DOM) rather than trusting that the code does what it looks like it does. That is how the −$317M scorecard, the phantom Fantastic Four, and the one-film Joker were all caught: by looking at the output instead of the source.

    None of this was in the plan. The plan was to check 50 numbers.
